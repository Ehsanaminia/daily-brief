Part two. Today's lesson. Funds and E T Fs.

Here is a problem. You want to own American stocks. The S and P 500 has five hundred companies. Do you buy five hundred separate shares, one by one? Of course not. It would cost a fortune, and you'd spend your life managing it.

The solution is a fund. A fund is a basket. Many investors put their money into one big pot. A professional, the asset manager from episode one, uses the pot to buy lots of different investments. And each investor owns a slice of the whole basket. So with one purchase, you own a tiny piece of hundreds of companies.

There are two main kinds of funds you will meet. The first is the mutual fund. You buy or sell it at one price, calculated once a day, after the markets close. The second is the E T F, which means exchange traded fund. An E T F is also a basket, but it trades on the stock exchange all day, just like a single share. You can buy it at ten in the morning and sell it at two in the afternoon, at the live price. That is why E T Fs became so popular. They are simple, flexible, and usually cheap.

Now, how does the basket decide what to buy? There are two styles. In passive investing, the fund simply copies an index. An S and P 500 fund holds the same five hundred companies as the index, in the same proportions. No one is trying to be clever. In active investing, a manager picks investments and tries to beat the index. Active funds usually charge more. Later, in week nine, we'll look at the evidence on whether the extra cost pays off. By the way, you already made a video about this: Do Pro Fund Managers Beat the Market?

Which brings us to fees, the most important number on any fund. The yearly fee is called the expense ratio. It is a percentage of your money, taken every year. Let's use numbers. You invest ten thousand dollars. A very cheap index fund might charge three hundredths of one percent. That is three dollars a year. An expensive active fund might charge one percent. That is one hundred dollars a year. Now stretch it over time. Suppose the market gives seven percent a year. With a one percent fee, you keep six. Over twenty years, ten thousand dollars grows to about thirty-eight thousand seven hundred without the fee, and only about thirty-two thousand one hundred with it. The fee costs you about six thousand six hundred dollars. That is an assumption, not a forecast, but it shows why your video, Does a One Percent Fee Really Matter, is such a good question.

And remember, a fund is not magic. If the market falls, the fund falls. On Friday, an S and P 500 fund fell about three point seven percent, just like the index. What a fund gives you is diversification. That is a big word with a simple meaning: don't put all your eggs in one basket. If one company in the fund fails, you lose only a tiny slice. We'll study it properly in week three.

Here is the part that connects back to your career. What does an asset manager actually sell? Funds. The fund is the product, and the fee is the income. When someone says they work in asset management, they work on creating, running, selling or analyzing funds like these. Now you know the whole picture of how that business works.
