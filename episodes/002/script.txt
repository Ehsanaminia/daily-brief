Part two. Today's lesson. What is a stock?

Imagine your friend has a small bakery. It is a good bakery, but she wants to open three more shops, and she doesn't have enough money. So she cuts the ownership of the bakery into one hundred equal slices, and she sells twenty of them to people, for fifty dollars each. Those slices are called shares. A share is a small piece of ownership in a company. And the word stock simply means shares of companies. So, if you own one slice, you own one percent of the bakery. You are a shareholder.

Why would anyone buy a slice? There are two ways to make money.

The first way is that the price of your slice goes up. If the bakery grows and earns more profit, other people want to buy a slice, and the price goes up. Say you bought for fifty dollars, and later you sell for eighty. You made thirty dollars.

The second way is a dividend. A dividend is a share of the profit that the company pays out to its owners, often every three months. Not every company pays one. But if your bakery has a good year, she might send each shareholder a few dollars.

Now the other side, and this part is important. A stock can go down, and sometimes it goes down a lot. If the bakery loses money, your slice is worth less. In the worst case, the bakery closes, and your slice can be worth nothing. Shareholders are paid last. First the bakery pays its workers, its suppliers, and its lenders. Only then does anything remain for the owners. That is why stocks have more risk than, for example, a bond. And in return for taking that risk, investors hope to earn more over the long run. Hope, not a promise.

How do people buy and sell? A big company's shares trade on a stock exchange. It is like a huge marketplace, but electronic. The company sells its shares to the public for the first time in what is called an IPO, short for initial public offering. After that, people just trade the shares with each other, all day, at a price that changes every second.

And why does a price move at all? Because it is a bet on the future. A share price is what people collectively think the company's future profits are worth. If news makes people more worried about those profits, the price falls. That is why, on Friday, technology stocks could fall five percent in a day without any company disappearing. Nothing physical changed. People simply changed their opinion about the future, and they changed it quickly.

So remember the key idea. A stock is a small piece of ownership in a real company. Its price reflects what people believe about the future, and it can go up or down.
