Part two. Today's lesson. What is a bond?

Let's go back to your friend with the bakery. Last time, she sold slices of ownership. But there is another way for her to raise money. She can borrow it. Say she borrows one thousand dollars from you, for five years. She promises two things. One: every year, she pays you fifty dollars as interest. Two: after five years, she gives you your one thousand dollars back. That is a bond. A bond is a loan that is packaged so it can be bought and sold.

Let me give you the three words every bond has. The first is the face value. That is the amount that gets paid back at the end. Here, one thousand dollars. The second is the coupon. That is the yearly interest payment, here fifty dollars, which is five percent of the face value. The third is the maturity. That is the date the loan ends and you get your money back. Here, five years from now. Face value, coupon, maturity. Say them with me once: face value, coupon, maturity.

Who issues bonds? Governments do, because they need to borrow to pay for roads, schools and so on. The American government's bonds are called Treasuries. Companies do as well. The company that borrows is called the issuer, and you, the lender, are the bondholder.

Compare this with a stock. A shareholder owns a piece of the company, and gets whatever is left. A bondholder is a lender, and has a contract. The company must pay the interest and the face value, whether it had a good year or a bad year. And if the company fails, lenders are paid before shareholders. That is why bonds are usually less risky than stocks. But less risky does not mean no risk. There are two risks to know today.

The first is credit risk. The borrower might not pay you back. A very strong government is unlikely to fail, so its bonds pay a lower rate. A weaker company pays a higher rate, to make up for the danger. A higher rate is the price of higher risk. That is a rule you will see everywhere.

The second risk is the one hidden in Friday's news, and it is the most important idea today. The price of a bond changes, and it moves opposite to the rate. Here is why.

You hold the bond that pays fifty dollars a year, bought for one thousand. Now imagine that interest rates in the world go up, and a brand new bond, just as safe, pays sixty dollars a year for one thousand. Would anyone pay you one thousand dollars for your bond that only pays fifty? No. If you want to sell, you must lower the price, until the buyer's deal looks as good as a new bond. So when rates go up, the price of your existing bond goes down.

Now the opposite. Rates fall, and new bonds pay only forty dollars a year. Your bond, which pays fifty, is now a nicer deal. Buyers will pay more than one thousand for it. So when rates go down, bond prices go up. Think of a see-saw. Rate on one side, price on the other. One goes up, the other goes down.

How big is the move? It depends on how long until maturity. Take our fifty dollar bond. If the market rate rises to six percent, a bond with five years left would be worth about nine hundred and fifty-eight dollars. But a bond with thirty years left would be worth only about eight hundred and sixty-two. Longer bonds move more. In week eight, we'll learn the professional word for this, which is duration.

Now go back to Friday. The ten year Treasury rate went from about five point oh five to four point nine one percent. The rate went down, so the price of those bonds went up, by roughly one percent. That is my rough estimate. So on a day when stocks fell almost four percent, government bonds went up a little. That is exactly why asset managers hold both stocks and bonds. When one falls, the other may cushion it. We will study that idea, called diversification, in week three.

And one last thing. If you hold the bond until maturity, you still get your face value back, whatever the price did in between. The price only matters if you sell early.
