Part two. Today's lesson. Interest.

Let's start with a real-life story. Your friend needs one thousand dollars today, and asks to borrow it from you for one year. You think: if I lend it, I can't use it myself for a year. And there is a chance my friend doesn't pay me back. So you say: fine, but you pay me fifty dollars extra. That fifty dollars is interest.

Interest is the price of borrowing money. That's the whole idea. When you rent an apartment, you pay rent for using someone's apartment. When you borrow money, you pay interest for using someone's money. The owner gets paid for waiting, and for the risk.

Why does it exist? Three reasons. One: waiting costs something. If you lend a thousand dollars, you can't spend it or invest it elsewhere in the meantime. Two: risk. The borrower might not pay you back. That is the credit risk from episode three. Three: prices in general tend to rise over time, so money loses buying power. We'll study that on Wednesday.

Now the rate. The interest rate is the interest, as a percentage of the amount, usually per year. Fifty dollars on a thousand dollars is five percent per year. The rate is a price, a price for money. And like every price, it is set by supply and demand: how many people want to borrow, and how many want to lend. A riskier borrower pays a higher rate. A safer borrower pays a lower one.

Who pays and who receives? The borrower pays interest, and the lender receives it. And here is the key insight: you are on both sides, every day. When you put money in a savings account, you are lending to the bank, and the bank pays you interest. When you borrow for a car or a house, you are the borrower, and you pay. A bank sits in the middle: it pays savers a lower rate, lends at a higher rate, and keeps the difference. When you buy a bond, you are the lender, and the coupon is your interest. The government or the company is the borrower.

Now, simple versus compound. Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. Take one thousand dollars at five percent. Each year, you earn fifty dollars. After three years, you have earned one hundred and fifty dollars. Compound interest means that you earn interest on the interest too. The first year is the same: fifty dollars. But in the second year, you earn five percent on one thousand and fifty, not on one thousand. After three years, you have earned about one hundred and fifty-eight dollars, not one hundred and fifty. A small difference now. Over decades, a very big one. That is tomorrow's episode, so I will leave it there.

Now the big question. Why do people say interest rates are the engine of finance? Because the rate is the price of money, and money is in everything. Let me connect it to what you already know.

First, bonds. In episode three you learned the see-saw: when rates go up, existing bond prices go down. Now you know why. A bond pays a fixed coupon. If new bonds start paying a higher rate, the old bond with the lower coupon is less attractive, so its price must fall until it competes. A bond is simply a loan, and its price follows the price of money.

Second, funds and fees. In episode four you saw that a one percent fee, taken every year, costs thousands over twenty years. That is the same math as interest, working against you. It's like paying a small interest rate to someone else, forever. Interest is everywhere, even where nobody calls it interest.

Third, stocks and everything else. A company that wants to build a factory often borrows the money. If the rate is high, borrowing is expensive, and fewer projects make sense. If the rate is low, more projects make sense. Mortgages, car loans, company loans and government debt all depend on this price. And professionals use the safe government rate, like that four point eight five percent, as a starting point. Every other investment must offer more than that to be worth the risk. If the safe government loan pays you almost five percent a year, why would you accept a risky investment that promises less? So the safe rate is the yardstick, and everything else is priced against it. We will come back to this many times.

One last point. Who controls the rate? Mostly, the market decides longer term rates, like the ten year. But the central bank, in America the Federal Reserve, sets a short term rate that influences all the others. Week five is about central banks, so we'll keep it simple for now.

So, to summarize. Interest is the price of borrowing money. The rate is that price, per year, as a percentage. The borrower pays, the lender receives. Riskier borrowers pay more. And almost every price in finance, from bonds to funds to stocks, is connected to it.
