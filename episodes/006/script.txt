Part two. Today's lesson. Interest, compounding, and real returns.

Start with the definition a professional uses. Interest is the price of money over time. The lender gives up money today and is paid for two things: waiting, and risk. The interest rate is that price as a percentage per year. Lend one thousand dollars at five percent, and you get fifty dollars a year. Like any price, it moves with supply and demand: how many want to borrow, how many want to lend. And a riskier borrower pays more. That extra is called a credit spread, and we'll meet it again in the bond weeks.

You are on both sides of this every day. A savings account is you lending to the bank. A mortgage is you borrowing. A bond is you lending to a government or a company, and the coupon is your interest. The bank in the middle pays savers less than it charges borrowers, and keeps the difference.

Now compounding. Simple interest is paid only on the original amount: one thousand dollars at five percent earns fifty dollars every year, so one hundred and fifty in three years. Compound interest means you also earn interest on past interest. Year one: fifty dollars. Year two: five percent of one thousand and fifty, so fifty-two dollars and fifty cents. After three years you have about one thousand one hundred and fifty-eight dollars, not one thousand one hundred and fifty. The formula is short: the future value equals today's amount times one plus the rate, raised to the number of years.

The small gap grows fast with time. At five percent, one thousand dollars becomes about one thousand six hundred and twenty-nine after ten years, and about four thousand three hundred and twenty after thirty years. With simple interest, thirty years would give only two thousand five hundred. That gap is why time in the market matters so much, and why a fee works against you the same way. In episode four you heard that a one percent yearly fee costs thousands over twenty years. That is compounding, running in reverse.

A quick shortcut professionals use: the rule of seventy-two. Divide seventy-two by the rate, and you get roughly the years it takes to double. At six percent, about twelve years. At nine percent, about eight. It is an approximation, close enough for a quick answer in a meeting.

Compounding frequency also matters. Five percent paid once a year is not the same as five percent paid monthly. Monthly, you earn a bit of interest on interest every month, so the effective yearly rate is about five point one two percent. That is why loans and savings products quote an effective annual rate: it lets you compare like with like.

Push it to the limit and you get continuous compounding, where interest is added every instant. Five percent compounded continuously gives an effective rate of about five point one three percent, only a hair more than monthly. You will see continuous rates in option pricing and in many risk models, because the math is cleaner. For now, just remember: the more often interest is added, the higher the effective rate, but the gains shrink quickly after monthly.

Here is a real-life check. A bank advertises a loan at five percent, and another at four point nine percent compounded monthly. Which is cheaper? Four point nine compounded monthly is about five point zero one percent effective. So the second loan is actually a touch more expensive. Always convert to the effective rate before comparing.

Now real returns. Money that grows five percent while prices grow three percent has only grown about two percent in what it can buy. That is the real return: roughly, the nominal return minus inflation. The exact version divides: one point zero five, divided by one point zero three, minus one, which is about one point nine four percent. The quick subtraction works when the numbers are small. When inflation is high, use the exact one.

Why does an asset manager care? Because a client doesn't eat nominal returns. A pension fund promises future payments in real terms. A bond paying four percent while inflation runs at five percent is losing buying power every year. In the bond weeks you'll meet bonds that protect against this, inflation-linked bonds.

Finally, the yardstick. The safe government rate, like that four point eight five percent on the ten year, is the return you can get with almost no credit risk. Every riskier investment has to promise more than that to be worth it. That's why a move in rates, like the drop of about thirty basis points you just heard, ripples into bonds, stocks and property. Who sets rates? The market sets longer rates like the ten year. The central bank, in America the Federal Reserve, sets a short-term rate that pulls on all the others. Central banks get their own week later.

What does a professional check, every time a rate is quoted? Four things. Is it simple or compounded, and how often? Is it nominal or real, so before or after inflation? Is it per year? And against what risk-free rate is it being compared? Get those four right, and you avoid most beginner mistakes with numbers.

To sum up. Interest is the price of money over time. Compounding is interest on interest, and it grows with time, for you or against you. The real return is what's left after inflation. And the safe rate is the yardstick for everything else.
